# Example: The Corporate Sponsorship Question

This is a fully worked, illustrative example — a fictional scenario for a mid-size environmental
nonprofit — showing the complete structure and tone an options paper should have. Read this to
understand what good looks like.

---

## Title block

> **The Corporate Sponsorship Question**
> Four options for engaging corporate partners without compromising campaign independence
>
> For: Leadership team
> From: Strategy team
> Date: [date]
> Status: Discussion draft

Note: the title names the tension ("The Corporate Sponsorship Question"), not the document type.
The subtitle says what the options do. The metadata is minimal — audience, author, date, status.

---

## Context

> Staff survey feedback this quarter identified corporate partnership policy as the most
> contentious open question in the five-year strategy. Two regional teams flagged it
> independently, from opposite directions — one wants clearer permission to pursue partners,
> the other wants a firmer line against them. This note sets out four framing options.

Note: two sentences. References specific evidence (staff survey, two teams raising it from
opposite directions). Gets straight to the point.

---

## The tension

> The organisation campaigns against specific industries for their environmental record, while
> its fundraising team is under pressure to diversify revenue beyond grants and individual
> giving. Corporate partnerships are a proven revenue channel for peer organisations — but taking
> money from the wrong company, even in an unrelated sector, risks a credibility hit that would
> outlast the funding many times over.
>
> Staff are split. One fundraiser put it plainly: "We can't out-fundraise our peers on
> individual giving alone — we need a partnerships model, and right now we don't have one."
> A campaigner countered just as plainly: "If we take money from any company that could be
> credibly accused of greenwashing by using our name, we've lost the argument before we've made
> it."

Note: names both sides of the tension. Uses an illustrative quote from each side. Doesn't try to
resolve it — just makes the reader feel why it matters.

---

## Options (condensed — showing structure, not full text)

### Option 1: The bright-line list
- **Core idea:** "We publish a standing exclusion list — sectors and named companies we will
  never take money from — and anything outside it is fair to consider."
- **How it works:** Leadership and campaigns jointly maintain a public list, reviewed annually.
  Fundraising can approach anyone not on it without a fresh debate each time.
- **Strengths:** Transparent. Predictable. Removes case-by-case litigation for the fundraising
  team.
- **Risks:** A list can't anticipate every edge case. Being seen to update it reactively (after
  criticism) looks worse than not having one.
- **Best for:** Giving fundraising a clear, defensible mandate to act. Needs pairing with a
  review mechanism for edge cases.

### Option 2: The independent review panel
- **Core idea:** "Any prospective corporate partnership above a threshold goes to a panel with
  external representation before it's accepted."
- **Strengths:** Distributes the reputational judgement call beyond one team. External members
  add credibility to "no" decisions and "yes" decisions alike.
- **Risks:** Slower. Requires recruiting and maintaining a credible panel. Can look like
  outsourcing a decision the organisation should own.
- **Best for:** High-value or ambiguous partnerships where a single team's judgement isn't
  enough cover.

### Option 3: The restricted-use fund
- **Core idea:** "Corporate money only ever funds ring-fenced operational costs — never campaign
  work — so no partner can be seen to be buying influence over what we say."
- **Strengths:** Structurally removes the "he who pays the piper" risk. Easier to defend
  publicly.
- **Risks:** Reduces how much of the funding actually helps the mission. Sophisticated critics
  may not accept the distinction anyway.
- **Best for:** Early, lower-trust partnerships, or as a standing rule alongside either option
  above.

### Option 4: Build the members' fund ★
- **Core idea:** "We invest in a mass-membership recurring-giving model instead — enough
  small-donor revenue that corporate partnerships stop being a financial necessity."
- **Strengths:** Removes the tension at the root rather than managing it. Broadens the
  organisation's base of support and its accountability.
- **Risks:** Slow to build. Requires upfront investment with no guaranteed return. Doesn't solve
  the near-term funding gap.
- **Best for:** The long-term aspiration. Doesn't answer what to do this year.

---

## Comparison table

| Option | Strength | Main risk | Operational cost | Best pairing |
|--------|----------|-----------|-----------------|--------------|
| 1. Bright-line list | Transparent, predictable | Can't cover every edge case | Low | Pair with 2 |
| 2. Review panel | Distributes judgement | Slower; recruiting overhead | Medium | Pair with 1 |
| 3. Restricted-use fund | Removes influence risk | Limits funding's impact | Low | Pair with 1 or 2 |
| 4. Members' fund ★ | Solves it at the root | Slow; no near-term relief | High | Pair with 3 |

---

## Suggested approach

> No single option resolves the tension on its own. A layered approach is likely strongest:
>
> - **Immediate layer:** Bright-line list (Option 1), so fundraising has a mandate now
> - **Safeguard layer:** Restricted-use fund (Option 3), so any partnership is structurally
>   low-risk from day one
> - **Escalation layer:** Review panel (Option 2), reserved for partnerships above a set
>   threshold
> - **Aspiration layer:** Members' fund (Option 4), pursued in parallel as the multi-year answer

Note: acknowledges they're complementary. Groups by function (immediate / safeguard / escalation
/ aspiration), not by preference order.

---

## Next steps

> - Draft a first version of the exclusion list with campaigns and fundraising together
> - Scope what "restricted use" would mean in practice for existing partnership conversations
> - Identify two or three candidates for an external review panel
> - Feed the members' fund business case into next year's strategy planning

Note: concrete, assigned where possible, flows from the suggested approach.

---

## What makes this paper work

1. **It names the tension honestly.** Doesn't hedge or minimise.
2. **Each option gets a fair hearing.** No straw men.
3. **The risks are real.** Not token caveats — genuine concerns that could derail the option.
4. **The comparison table makes trade-offs visible** at a glance.
5. **The suggested approach is a suggestion,** not a fait accompli.
6. **Option names are memorable.** "The bright-line list" sticks in your head.
7. **The ★ option stretches thinking** without being irresponsible.
